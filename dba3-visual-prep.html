Isolation, Intervention & Empire

Quick contextYou are a newspaper editor in 1890s America. The nation is debating whether to expand overseas, control Hawaii and Cuba, or stay focused on domestic issues. Business leaders want markets; some politicians want glory; others want to help; and many Americans just want to stay out of foreign conflicts.
understand

Explain the difference between isolation, intervention, and imperialism. Give one example of each from U.S. history before WWI.

How to approach this:
Start by defining each term clearly, then give a specific historical example for each from before 1914. Show that you can tell them apart.
analyze

American leaders said imperialism spread civilization and opened markets. People in Cuba, the Philippines, and Panama had their own views. Whose perspective does the evidence support more strongly?

How to approach this:
Look at what actually happened in Cuba, the Philippines, and Panama. Did conditions improve under U.S. control? Did local people agree with the American justifications?
evaluate

Does a powerful nation have the right to control weaker ones if it claims to be helping them? What is the strongest argument for each side?

How to approach this:
Take a clear position. Then name the strongest argument on the other side and explain why you still disagree with it.
EOC Practice Question

Which factor best explains why the United States pursued an imperial policy in the 1890s?

Correct Answer: B

Why this is correct: American imperialism in the 1890s was driven by a combination of motives: industrialists wanted new markets and raw materials, naval strategists like Alfred Thayer Mahan argued for overseas bases and a powerful fleet, and many Americans believed in ideas like Social Darwinism and the "duty" to spread American civilization. No single factor alone explains annexations like Hawaii or the acquisition of the Philippines; it was this combination.

Why the other answers are historically incorrect:
A: Missionary activity was real but was one strand among many, not the primary driver of official policy.

C: Territorial expansion was never put to a national popular vote; decisions were made by presidents, Congress, and treaty negotiations, and anti-imperialists strongly opposed them.

D: European powers did not force American expansion; if anything, the U.S. acted to compete with European empires, as in the Open Door policy toward China.

U.S. Entry into WWI

Quick contextYou are in Congress, 1917. Europe is bleeding from four years of brutal war. Americans have stayed out, but events are pushing the nation toward involvement. President Wilson asks you to vote on a declaration of war. What were the reasons, and was this the right choice?
understand

Name two events or factors that moved the U.S. from neutrality toward entering WWI.

How to approach this:
Name two different factors (one military event, one economic or political) that made Americans reconsider neutrality. Be specific: which events actually shifted opinion?
analyze

The U.S. claimed neutrality but sold supplies to the Allies and objected to German submarine warfare. Germany sank ships to cut off those supplies. Who was actually neutral, and who had the stronger argument?

How to approach this:
Wilson said America wasn't truly neutral because it supported the Allies with supplies. Germany said that made American ships fair targets. Which side makes the stronger argument based on the facts?
create

You are a senator in 1916. The U.S. can stay neutral, quietly support the Allies, or enter the war openly. Each choice has real costs in lives, money, and alliances. Which do you choose and why?

How to approach this:
Pick the choice that seems wisest given the actual risks and benefits. Name what you gain with that choice, what you lose, and why the tradeoff is worth making.
EOC Practice Question

What was the primary reason the United States entered World War I in 1917?

Correct Answer: B

Why this is correct: Unrestricted submarine warfare by Germany killed Americans and threatened American ships carrying supplies to Britain. The Zimmermann Telegram (a secret German message proposing an alliance with Mexico against the U.S.) was intercepted and shocked American public opinion. These events made continued neutrality impossible; Congress voted for war in April 1917.

Why the other answers are historically incorrect:
A: Economic interests in supporting Britain mattered, but the immediate cause was German submarine attacks and the Zimmermann Telegram.

C: Wilson ran for re-election in 1916 on "He kept us out of war." Entering war was not his original goal.

D: American banks and businesses traded with both sides; economic ties alone did not force America to choose.

Aftermath: Treaty & League

Quick contextYou are in the Versailles peace negotiations, 1919. The war is over; millions are dead; the world must rebuild. President Wilson wants a fair, lasting peace. European leaders want revenge and security. The United States can help build a new world order or stay out. What's the right call?
understand

Name two specific ways the Treaty of Versailles differed from Wilson's 14 Points.

How to approach this:
Compare Wilson's 14 Points to the actual Treaty. Name two ways the treaty was harsher or more punitive than what Wilson wanted. Be specific about what changed.
analyze

Wilson's 14 Points called for self-determination and a League of Nations. The Treaty of Versailles punished Germany harshly and ignored many of Wilson's principles. What happened between the vision and the result?

How to approach this:
Explain why the final treaty looked so different from Wilson's vision. Who had more power in the negotiations? What happened to idealism when it met reality?
evaluate

The Senate refused to join the League of Nations. Was that refusal wise, short-sighted, or somewhere in between? What does America's absence mean for how the League functioned?

How to approach this:
Was the Senate right to reject the League? Take a position and defend it with evidence. What would have happened if America had joined?
EOC Practice Question

How did the Treaty of Versailles differ from Wilson's 14 Points?

Correct Answer: C

Why this is correct: Wilson's 14 Points called for a peace based on self-determination, freedom of seas, and fair treatment of all nations. The Treaty of Versailles, shaped by British and French demands, imposed harsh reparations, territorial losses, and punitive measures on Germany. This contradicted Wilson's vision of a peace that would prevent future conflict by treating nations fairly.

Why the other answers are historically incorrect:
A: The opposite is true. Wilson wanted fairness; Versailles was harsh and punitive toward Germany.

B: Wilson actually championed the League of Nations, which was included in the Treaty.

D: The League of Nations was established as part of the Treaty of Versailles, though the U.S. Senate later rejected membership.
