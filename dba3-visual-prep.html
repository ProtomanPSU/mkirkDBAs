Isolation, Intervention & Empire

Quick context After decades of relative isolation, the U.S. began building an overseas empire in the 1890s through the Spanish-American War, acquiring the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam. American leaders justified expansion through ideas about civilization, markets, and national destiny — while those under U.S. control often saw it differently.
Primary Source
The American people are not opposed to the expansion of our borders. Every dollar of trade with Cuba, Hawaii, and the Philippines is a dollar earned. Empire is commerce, and commerce is prosperity.
American imperialist argument, 1890s
Question 1: How does this writer connect empire to commerce? What is left out of this argument?
Question 2: What about the people already living in Cuba, Hawaii, and the Philippines? Whose interests are represented?
Myth:
American imperialism was about spreading democracy and civilization to backward peoples.
Reality:
American expansion was driven by economic interest: markets, resources, and strategic military bases. The "civilizing mission" was ideology used to justify what was fundamentally extractive. The Philippines, Hawaii, and Puerto Rico became colonies that enriched American corporations while their populations had limited say in their own governance.
Interpretation 1
American expansion into the Caribbean and Pacific was a natural extension of continental expansion. It reflected the era's competitive imperialism among world powers. The US was not uniquely brutal—it was participating in a system all major powers were engaged in.
Some historians contextualize American imperialism within the broader age of imperialism.
Interpretation 2
American imperialism was extractive colonialism. The US military overthrew democratic governments (Hawaii), fought brutal wars to suppress independence movements (Philippines), and established economies dependent on American corporations. The "civilizing mission" masked economic domination.
Anti-imperialist historians emphasize the perspectives of colonized peoples and the disruption imperialism caused.

U.S. Entry into WWI

Quick context The U.S. declared neutrality in 1914 but sold supplies to the Allies and protested German submarine warfare. The sinking of the Lusitania, the Zimmermann Telegram, and unrestricted submarine warfare gradually made neutrality untenable, and the U.S. entered the war in April 1917.
Primary Source
The sinking of the Lusitania was an act of terrorism against American citizens. We cannot remain neutral in the face of such German aggression. American honor demands a response.
American newspaper editorial, 1915
Question 1: What does the writer mean by "American honor"? How does honor justify military action?
Question 2: Britain had also killed American sailors. Why was German action called terrorism but British action was acceptable?
Myth:
America entered World War I because the US was attacked by Germany and had to defend itself.
Reality:
America was never attacked by Germany. German submarines sank ships carrying American cargo and passengers, but this was not an attack on American territory or a declaration of war against the US. America entered the war for strategic reasons: to preserve a profitable trading relationship with Britain and France, to prevent German hegemony in Europe, and to maintain American power in a post-war world.
Interpretation 1
American entry into WWI was necessary to prevent German domination of Europe and preserve the international balance of power. Remaining neutral would have allowed German militarism to triumph, leading to an even worse outcome.
Many historians argue American intervention prevented catastrophic German victory.
Interpretation 2
America entered a war that did not require American participation. The war could have ended through negotiation. American intervention killed millions, destabilized Europe, and created the conditions for an even more brutal conflict 20 years later.
Historians skeptical of the war argue it was economically motivated, not strategically necessary.

Aftermath: Treaty & League

Quick context Wilson's 14 Points promised a just peace based on self-determination and a League of Nations, but the Treaty of Versailles punished Germany harshly with reparations and territorial losses. The U.S. Senate refused to ratify the treaty, leaving Wilson's League without its most powerful potential member.
Primary Source
The Great War took my leg and my brother's life. We were told it would make the world safe for democracy. But I came home to a country that treated us as disposable. The factories that made the weapons that won the war now laid us off. There is no gratitude in victory.
World War I veteran testimony, 1920s
Question 1: What contrast is the veteran describing? What did he expect vs. what he received?
Question 2: This veteran was told the war would "make the world safe for democracy." What happened to that promise?
Myth:
World War I soldiers were treated as heroes and rewarded for their sacrifice.
Reality:
Returning soldiers faced unemployment, inadequate medical care for wounds and shell shock, and cultural indifference. The postwar economy had no jobs for millions of veterans. Government support was minimal. The 1932 Bonus Army march, where veterans demanded promised benefits, was met with violence from federal troops.
Interpretation 1
World War I transformed America from a regional power into a global superpower. The war created an industrial capacity that dominated the world economy, established American military superiority, and positioned the US to shape the post-war order. These were significant strategic achievements.
Many diplomatic and military historians emphasize American ascendancy after the war.
Interpretation 2
World War I killed 120,000 American soldiers, created generational trauma, and achieved little. The League of Nations failed. Europe descended into the Great Depression. The promised "peace" collapsed into the conditions that would create World War II. The sacrifice produced nothing but debt and disillusionment.
Historians emphasizing human cost argue the war was a strategic failure despite industrial gains.

Name two specific ways the Treaty of Versailles differed from Wilson's 14 Points.

How to approach this: Focus on concrete differences — the war guilt clause, the size of reparations, the rejection of self-determination for certain peoples. Each difference you name should be something you can explain, not just list.
analyze

Wilson's 14 Points called for self-determination and a League of Nations. The Treaty of Versailles punished Germany harshly and ignored many of Wilson's principles. What happened between the vision and the result?

How to approach this: Track the gap: what did Wilson want, what did Britain and France want, and how did the negotiating dynamics produce a result neither fully intended? That gap is where the historical explanation lives.
evaluate

The Senate refused to join the League of Nations. Was that refusal wise, short-sighted, or somewhere in between? What does America's absence mean for how the League functioned?

How to approach this: Take a position and follow its consequences. If you say the refusal was wise, explain what the U.S. avoided. If you say it was short-sighted, explain what the League lost without U.S. membership.
