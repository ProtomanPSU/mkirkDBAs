U.S. & the Middle East / Reagan Era

Quick context The 1970s oil crisis, the Iran Hostage Crisis, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan drew the U.S. deeper into Middle Eastern affairs. Reagan won the 1980 election on promises of tax cuts, deregulation, and Cold War resolve — policies that grew the economy and the deficit simultaneously while reshaping the Democratic-Republican divide.
Primary Source
Government is not the solution to our problem; government IS the problem. From time to time, we have been tempted to believe that society has become too complex to manage ourselves. But if no one among us is capable of governing himself, then who among us has the capacity to govern someone else?
Ronald Reagan, Inaugural Address, 1981
Question 1: What is Reagan saying about the role of government? What does he propose instead?
Question 2: Is this claim accurate—that government causes more problems than it solves?
Myth:
Conservative ideology won in the 1980s because Americans genuinely preferred less government and lower taxes.
Reality:
Conservative ideology won through political organization, media messaging, and coalition-building. Surveys showed Americans wanted both lower taxes AND more government services. The victory was ideological, not reflective of unified public preference for a particular policy agenda.
Interpretation 1
By the 1970s, government had become bloated and inefficient. High taxes and regulation were constraining economic growth. Conservative reforms in the 1980s restored economic dynamism and American power globally. The resulting growth improved living standards.
Conservative historians and economists credit Reagan with economic revival.
Interpretation 2
Conservative policies in the 1980s reduced taxes on the wealthy, weakened unions and worker power, and eliminated regulations protecting environment and workers. The result was rising inequality, stagnant wages for workers, and a transfer of wealth to the top 1%.
Critical historians argue conservatism increased inequality and weakened the middle class.

Reagan's economic policies (Reaganomics) promised growth through tax cuts and deregulation. Who benefited most from these policies, and who did not? What does that distribution reveal?

How to approach this: Name specific groups on both sides — upper-income earners, financial sector versus industrial workers, rural communities. Then draw the larger conclusion: what economic theory was being tested, and what did the results show?
evaluate

The Iran-Contra affair showed that the executive branch was conducting secret foreign policy that bypassed Congress. Does national security justify that kind of secrecy? What is the strongest argument on each side?

How to approach this: Present both arguments genuinely. The strongest pro-secrecy argument isn't just 'national security' — it's about operational necessity and adversarial intelligence. The strongest anti-secrecy argument isn't just 'laws were broken' — it's about democratic accountability and precedent.

Terrorism, Human Rights & Nuclear Threats

Quick context The September 11 attacks triggered the PATRIOT Act, the Department of Homeland Security, the invasion of Afghanistan, and later Iraq. These decisions launched debates about civil liberties, torture, indefinite detention, and the limits of executive power that continued across multiple presidencies.
Primary Source
The Soviet Union has collapsed. American values have triumphed. We are now the sole superpower. History has ended. Liberal democracy and capitalism have won.
Francis Fukuyama, "The End of History," 1989-1992
Question 1: What does Fukuyama mean by "the end of history"? What has won?
Question 2: How has this prediction aged? Has history actually ended?
Myth:
The Cold War's end meant America could reduce military spending and focus on domestic needs.
Reality:
Military spending remained high after 1991. The US fought new wars in Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria, and elsewhere. The Pentagon budget remained massive even without a Soviet threat. The "peace dividend" never materialized.
Interpretation 1
The US Cold War victory left America as the dominant global power. This victory was deserved and reflected the superiority of democratic capitalism. The post-Cold War order, led by America, provided unprecedented global stability and growth.
Many historians celebrate the Cold War victory and American dominance.
Interpretation 2
American triumphalism after the Cold War led to reckless interventions, military overreach, and the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. This hubris damaged American credibility and led to decades of costly, unsuccessful wars. The victory was squandered through arrogance.
Critical historians argue American hubris after 1991 led to strategic disasters.

How to approach this: Pick one with enough substance to discuss — the PATRIOT Act (surveillance to detect terrorism), the invasion of Afghanistan (destroy al-Qaeda and Taliban), Guantanamo Bay (detain enemy combatants outside normal legal jurisdiction). After 9/11, the U.S. passed the Patriot Act, created the Department of Homeland Security, and opened Guantanamo Bay. Which of these responses do you think most strained civil liberties, and why?

How to approach this: Pick one and explain the specific civil liberty it affected — Fourth Amendment searches, habeas corpus, free association. The strongest answer traces a specific legal or policy consequence, not just a general concern.

How to approach this: Name your recommendation and the principle behind it — deterrence, non-proliferation norms, proportionality. Then acknowledge the biggest risk of your chosen option and explain why it's still preferable to the alternatives.

Environmental, Social & Health Issues

Quick context Modern America has faced overlapping domestic crises: the HIV/AIDS epidemic, the opioid crisis, COVID-19, and climate change — each revealing gaps between public health needs and government capacity or political will. Globalization reshaped the economy, creating new wealth while displacing manufacturing workers and communities.
Primary Source
We were told that freedom and democracy were spreading. Instead, we have seen endless wars, surveillance, inequality worse than at any time since the 1920s, and a political system that feels broken. The promises of the American system are not being kept.
Contemporary American citizen reflection, 2020s
Question 1: What gap is this person describing between promise and reality?
Question 2: Is this assessment accurate? What evidence supports or contradicts it?
Myth:
America's global dominance has been challenged only recently by China and Russia.
Reality:
American power has been declining in relative terms for decades. The US won the Cold War but has not won subsequent wars. China has been rising for 40 years. American political polarization and institutional dysfunction are not new but have deepened.
Interpretation 1
America remains the world's dominant power with the largest economy, strongest military, and most innovative culture. Challenges are real but surmountable. American institutions are resilient and the nation will adapt and emerge stronger.
Many historians and analysts remain confident in American resilience and adaptability.
Interpretation 2
America is experiencing genuine decline: relative economic power shrinking, military overextended, political institutions polarized and dysfunctional, inequality destabilizing the middle class, and global influence waning. Recovery requires fundamental restructuring.
Historians documenting relative decline warn of structural problems requiring major reform.

Name one environmental or public health crisis from modern American history and describe how the government initially responded.

How to approach this: Pick one you know well enough to discuss the response specifically — the HIV/AIDS crisis (slow federal action under Reagan), the opioid epidemic (pharmaceutical industry oversight failures), COVID-19 (federal-state coordination breakdowns). Initial response is the key phrase — not the eventual outcome.
analyze

The HIV/AIDS crisis, the opioid epidemic, and COVID-19 all exposed gaps between public health needs and government response. What patterns do you see across these three crises?

How to approach this: Look for structural patterns, not just surface similarities — delayed response, politics overriding science, uneven impact across race and class. The strongest answers name the pattern precisely and give evidence from at least two of the three crises.
evaluate

Globalization, trade agreements, and immigration have all been described as both opportunities and threats to American workers. Using a specific example, argue which description is more accurate and why.

How to approach this: Pick a specific trade agreement (NAFTA) or industry (manufacturing in the Midwest) and trace its actual effects. Then take a position: on balance, did this specific example create more opportunity or more threat, and for whom?
