LBJ's Great Society

Quick contextYou are an activist in the 1960s. President Johnson is pushing major legislation on civil rights, poverty, education, and healthcare. But there's a backlash from conservatives and increasing resistance to government programs. Is this the right direction for America?
understand

Name one Great Society program from each category: civil rights legislation, education or arts, and environmental or consumer protection. Why did Johnson push all three at once?

How to approach this:
Explain what 'separate but equal' meant under Plessy v. Ferguson and why Brown v. Board overturned it.
analyze

How does the Great Society compare to the New Deal? What problems was each trying to solve, and what did each assume government could and should do?

How to approach this:
Brown said integration was required, but the South resisted massively. Why was legal victory not enough? What else was needed?
evaluate

Some Great Society programs created lasting institutions; others faced criticism for overreach or unintended consequences. Using a specific example, argue whether government expansion in this era did more good or harm.

How to approach this:
Was the court the right place to start the civil rights movement, or should activists have focused elsewhere first?
EOC Practice Question

Why was the Brown v. Board of Education decision considered a turning point?

Civil Rights Movement: People & Protests

Quick contextYou are a young activist in the 1950s and 1960s. The Civil Rights Movement is building. You see peaceful sit-ins met with violence, freedom marches blocked, voting rights denied. How do you fight for change? What tactics work? What are you willing to sacrifice?
understand

Name one specific Civil Rights protest or event and describe what it accomplished or revealed.

How to approach this:
Explain nonviolent direct action. Name specific tactics: sit-ins, freedom rides, marches, boycotts.
analyze

Pick two key figures or organizations from the Civil Rights Movement. How were their strategies different? Did those differences help or hurt the movement's overall goals?

How to approach this:
Why was nonviolence so powerful? How did peaceful protesters responding to violence change people's minds?
create

You face legal segregation and can choose to: accept it quietly, protest nonviolently and risk arrest, push back with more confrontational tactics, or relocate. What would you choose? What are the costs and what does your choice depend on?

How to approach this:
Name two key civil rights leaders and explain their different approaches to fighting segregation.
EOC Practice Question

What was the strategic value of nonviolent protests like sit-ins and freedom rides?

Correct Answer: C

Why this is correct: Nonviolent direct action created powerful moral contrast. When peaceful protesters were met with violence from police and mobs, public opinion often shifted. Media coverage of brutality against nonviolent citizens pressured politicians to act.

Why the other answers are historically incorrect:
A: While violence did occur, nonviolence was strategically designed to win sympathy, not to provoke and justify harsh response.

B: Nonviolent protests proved highly effective; they led to major legislative victories like the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

D: Nonviolent tactics were central to achieving civil rights goals; they generated public sympathy and political pressure.

Other Social Movements & the Courts

Quick contextYou are observing America in the 1960s and 1970s. The civil rights movement opened doors for other groups: women, Hispanics, Native Americans, people with disabilities. The courts are being used to expand rights. What's the impact of all these movements on American society?
understand

Name one social movement that emerged alongside or after the Civil Rights Movement and identify one specific goal it fought for.

How to approach this:
Explain what the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act actually did. What did each law make illegal?
analyze

The Chicano Movement, Native American Power Movement, and Women's Movement all organized separately from the Civil Rights Movement. What did they share in goals and strategy, and why did they choose to organize independently?

How to approach this:
These laws were victories, but racism continued after 1965. Why wasn't legal equality enough to create actual equality?
evaluate

Supreme Court cases in this era expanded student rights, suspects' rights, and reproductive rights. Pick one and argue whether the Court's decision made America more just, or whether it overstepped its proper role.

How to approach this:
Could the civil rights movement have succeeded with violence instead? What would have been different?
EOC Practice Question

How did the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965 change American law?

Correct Answer: B

Why this is correct: Brown v. Board overturned Plessy v. Ferguson by declaring separate schools inherently unequal. Though implementation was difficult and slow, it provided legal foundation for the civil rights movement.

Why the other answers are historically incorrect:
A: Brown provided the legal foundation for desegregation, but implementation took decades; segregation was not immediately ended.

C: Brown explicitly rejected 'separate but equal' and ruled against segregation.

D: Brown had profound effects; it energized the civil rights movement and led to further court challenges and legislative action.
