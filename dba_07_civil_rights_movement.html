LBJ's Great Society

Quick context Lyndon Johnson used his landslide 1964 election and political skills to pass the most ambitious domestic legislation since the New Deal — the Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, Medicare, Medicaid, Head Start, and the National Endowment for the Arts, among others. Vietnam ultimately consumed his presidency and much of its political capital.
understand

Name one Great Society program from each category: civil rights legislation, education or arts, and environmental or consumer protection. Why did Johnson push all three at once?

How to approach this: Pick specific programs, not just the categories. Then explain the political logic: Johnson had a brief window of enormous congressional majorities after 1964 — he knew it wouldn't last.
analyze

How does the Great Society compare to the New Deal? What problems was each trying to solve, and what did each assume government could and should do?

How to approach this: Don't just list programs — compare assumptions. What did FDR think government was for? What did LBJ think? Where did their visions overlap and where did they differ?
evaluate

Some Great Society programs created lasting institutions; others faced criticism for overreach or unintended consequences. Using a specific example, argue whether government expansion in this era did more good or harm.

How to approach this: Pick one program and follow its actual history. Medicare is still running. The War on Poverty had mixed results. Pick something specific and trace it — that specificity is what makes the argument credible.

Civil Rights Movement: People & Protests

Quick context The Civil Rights Movement drew on churches, student organizations, and legal advocacy to challenge segregation through boycotts, sit-ins, freedom rides, and marches. Internal debates about nonviolence versus more confrontational tactics, and about integration versus Black Power, shaped the movement's direction throughout the 1950s and 60s.
understand

Name one specific Civil Rights protest or event and describe what it accomplished or revealed.

How to approach this: Pick something you can explain fully — the Montgomery Bus Boycott, the Greensboro sit-ins, the March on Birmingham. Then say what it actually changed or exposed, not just that it 'raised awareness.'
analyze

Pick two key figures or organizations from the Civil Rights Movement. How were their strategies different? Did those differences help or hurt the movement's overall goals?

How to approach this: Be specific — MLK and SNCC, or NAACP and CORE. Name a concrete strategic difference, not just tone. Then make a real argument: did the diversity of strategies expand the movement's reach, or create confusion about its goals?
create

You face legal segregation and can choose to: accept it quietly, protest nonviolently and risk arrest, push back with more confrontational tactics, or relocate. What would you choose? What are the costs and what does your choice depend on?

How to approach this: Don't pick the heroic option automatically. What are you risking — your job, your family's safety, your community? The most honest answers name real stakes and explain what factors would actually determine the choice.

Other Social Movements & the Courts

Quick context The Civil Rights Movement inspired the Chicano Movement, the American Indian Movement, the Women's Liberation Movement, and the LGBTQ+ rights movement — each drawing on its tactics and language while addressing their own distinct conditions. The Warren Court expanded rights through landmark cases including Miranda v. Arizona, Engel v. Vitale, and Roe v. Wade.
understand

Name one social movement that emerged alongside or after the Civil Rights Movement and identify one specific goal it fought for.

How to approach this: Pick one — Chicano Movement (farm worker rights, bilingual education), AIM (treaty rights, sovereignty), Women's Liberation (Equal Pay Act, Title IX). One movement, one concrete goal, stated specifically.
analyze

The Chicano Movement, Native American Power Movement, and Women's Movement all organized separately from the Civil Rights Movement. What did they share in goals and strategy, and why did they choose to organize independently?

How to approach this: Look for the pattern: shared tactics (marches, legal challenges, political pressure) but distinct grievances. Why did each group need its own movement rather than joining a unified coalition?
evaluate

Supreme Court cases in this era expanded student rights, suspects' rights, and reproductive rights. Pick one and argue whether the Court's decision made America more just, or whether it overstepped its proper role.

How to approach this: Pick a case you can discuss specifically — Miranda, Tinker v. Des Moines, Roe. State the holding, then argue whether the Constitution actually supported it or whether the Court was making policy. Your teacher will ask which side of the debate you find more convincing.
