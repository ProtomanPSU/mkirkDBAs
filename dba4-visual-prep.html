Culture, Prosperity & Tensions

Quick context The 1920s brought jazz, flappers, consumer goods, and the Harlem Renaissance, but also the Red Scare, the KKK revival, immigration restrictions, and Prohibition. Prosperity was real but uneven — farmers and industrial workers rarely shared in the boom that stock investors and urban consumers experienced.
Primary Source
American prosperity is not ephemeral. The fundamental conditions underlying it are sound. Stock prices are reasonable given the earnings power of American business. The outlook is bright.
Irving Fisher, Yale economist, 1929
Question 1: What is Fisher saying about stock prices? What is his prediction?
Question 2: This was written weeks before the stock market crashed 50%. What was Fisher wrong about?
Myth:
The 1920s were prosperous for all Americans. Everyone enjoyed wealth and freedom.
Reality:
The 1920s created new prosperity for the urban middle class and wealthy, but agricultural America remained poor. Workers' wages did not keep pace with productivity gains. Stock market wealth was concentrated: in 1929, the top 1% owned 44% of stocks. The prosperity was unequally distributed and built on debt, not sustainable growth.
Interpretation 1
The 1920s represented genuine progress: technological innovation, cultural dynamism, rising living standards for millions. The decade produced the automobile, radio, cinema, and new forms of entertainment and social freedom. These were real achievements.
Cultural historians often celebrate the era's creativity and modernization.
Interpretation 2
The 1920s were characterized by a dangerous illusion of prosperity built on easy credit, stock speculation, and unsustainable inequality. Working people were excluded from the gains. The economy was fundamentally fragile, waiting to collapse.
Economic historians point to structural weaknesses that made the collapse inevitable.

How to approach this: The strongest answers name a specific group — tenant farmers in the South, textile workers in New England — and explain the structural reason they were excluded, not just that they were poor.

Fundamentalists resisted the cultural changes of the 1920s while cities embraced them. Was that resistance reasonable, fearful, or both? What were they trying to protect?

How to approach this: Avoid dismissing one side. What did traditionalists actually fear losing? What did modernists actually believe they were gaining? Then say which argument you find more persuasive and why.

Causes & Crash

Quick context The Great Depression was not caused by the stock market crash alone — it followed years of overproduction, easy credit, speculative investment, and weak banking regulations. When the market collapsed in October 1929, it triggered bank failures, unemployment, and a contraction that spread worldwide.
Primary Source
I had saved $3,000. I put it all in the market on the advice of a man who said stock prices only go up. In three months, it was worth $300. My family lost our home. I could not look my wife in the eye. I still cannot.
Depression-era account, collected oral history
Question 1: What was this person's investment strategy? Why did they believe it was safe?
Question 2: How much of the Depression's impact fell on individuals who had no say in financial policy decisions?
Myth:
The Great Depression happened because people made bad personal financial decisions.
Reality:
The Depression was a systemic crisis caused by bank failures, speculative excess, the Federal Reserve's tight money policy, and the loss of purchasing power among workers. Individual people lost savings not because of personal failure but because of macro-economic forces beyond their control. Blaming individuals ignores the structural causes.
Interpretation 1
The Federal Reserve's contraction of money supply turned a recession into the Great Depression. If the Fed had maintained money supply, the downturn would have been much milder. The crisis was a policy failure, not inevitable.
Milton Friedman and other monetarist economists emphasize central bank responsibility.
Interpretation 2
The Depression reflected fundamental structural problems: inequality so severe that workers could not sustain consumption, bank fragility, and excessive speculation. No single policy could have prevented it. The economy needed fundamental reform.
Institutional economists emphasize the need for systemic change, not just policy tweaks.

The stock market crash didn't happen randomly. It followed years of speculation, easy credit, and overproduction. Which cause do you think was most responsible for making the crash inevitable? Why?

How to approach this: Pick one cause and trace it forward: how did it create the conditions for the others to become catastrophic? The strongest answer shows a causal chain, not just a list.

How to approach this: Make the 1928 choice first — with what people knew then, not what we know now. Then explain the 1932 view. The point is showing why smart people made decisions that look reckless in hindsight.

New Deal & Government Response

Quick context FDR's New Deal launched dozens of programs to provide relief, recovery, and reform — from the Civilian Conservation Corps to Social Security. Critics on the right called it socialist overreach; critics on the left said it didn't go far enough. The Depression didn't fully end until wartime spending in the 1940s.
Primary Source
The test of our progress is not whether we add more to the abundance of those who have much. It is whether we provide enough for those who have too little. I see one-third of a nation ill-housed, ill-clad, ill-nourished. It is my task to cure this.
Franklin D. Roosevelt, Second Inaugural Address, 1937
Question 1: What problem is FDR identifying? Whose interests is he claiming to represent?
Question 2: Did the New Deal actually solve this problem? What happened after 1937?
Myth:
The New Deal ended the Great Depression and put America back to work.
Reality:
The New Deal provided relief and some employment, but unemployment remained above 10% until World War II. The programs were significant but not sufficient to end the Depression. What actually ended it was massive military spending and war production after 1941.
Interpretation 1
The New Deal saved the country from collapse. It prevented starvation and homelessness, kept the banking system functioning, and created jobs that rebuilt American infrastructure. Without it, the social and political consequences would have been catastrophic.
Most mainstream historians credit the New Deal with preventing worse outcomes and preserving democratic institutions.
Interpretation 2
The New Deal was too timid. FDR refused to challenge capitalism's fundamental structure. He rejected proposals for wealth redistribution, universal healthcare, or full employment guarantee. He stabilized the system but did not transform it. The Depression could have been the moment for real change.
Left-wing and radical historians argue FDR missed the opportunity for fundamental economic reform.

Some opposed the New Deal because they believed it gave too much power to the federal government. Others said it didn't go far enough. What is the strongest version of each criticism?

How to approach this: Don't pick the weak version of either argument. What is the most compelling conservative case? The most compelling progressive case? Name a specific program as your example for each.
evaluate

The New Deal had long-term effects on workers' rights, government size, and presidential power. Which effect do you think shaped American life most durably, and why?

How to approach this: Pick one and build a case that it outlasted the others. The strongest answers point to something still visible in American life today — a program, an expectation, a legal structure.
