Origins & Truman's Response

Quick context After WWII, the U.S. and USSR emerged as rival superpowers with incompatible ideologies and competing visions for Europe's future. Truman responded with the Truman Doctrine (aid to countries resisting communism), the Marshall Plan (economic recovery for Western Europe), and NATO (a mutual defense alliance).
understand

What were the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, and NATO, and how did each one try to contain Soviet influence?

How to approach this: Know each one specifically: Truman Doctrine is military/political aid, Marshall Plan is economic reconstruction, NATO is collective military defense. Then explain how all three worked as parts of a single containment strategy.
analyze

Americans said the Soviets wanted to spread communism everywhere. Soviets said America was encircling them with military alliances. Which argument has more support from the evidence, or do both have merit?

How to approach this: Don't just say 'both sides had a point' — weigh them. Which fear was more grounded in actual actions? Use specific examples: Soviet behavior in Eastern Europe, U.S. bases in Western Europe.
evaluate

Could the U.S. and USSR have found a way to coexist peacefully after WWII, or was conflict structurally unavoidable given their ideologies and rival interests?

How to approach this: Take a clear position. If you say avoidable, name a specific decision point where a different choice could have changed the trajectory. If you say unavoidable, explain which structural factor made cooperation impossible.

Crises, Korea & Vietnam

Quick context The Cold War turned hot in Korea (1950-53) and Vietnam (escalating through the 1960s), while nuclear near-misses like the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) showed how close the superpowers came to direct war. Both Korea and Vietnam ended inconclusively, raising questions about the limits of containment.
analyze

The U.S. had missiles in Turkey aimed at the USSR; the Soviets placed missiles in Cuba aimed at the U.S. Why did America treat these situations so differently? What does that reveal about perspective and threat perception?

How to approach this: The geography matters — but so does framing. Why was Cuba seen as an existential threat when Turkey was presented as defensive? The strongest answer explains the asymmetry without just saying 'hypocrisy.'
create

You are advising the president during the Cuban Missile Crisis. Options include an air strike, a naval blockade, secret negotiations, or a public ultimatum. Each risks nuclear war in a different way. What do you recommend, and what are you willing to risk?

How to approach this: Pick one option and explain the specific risk you're accepting. Don't say 'blockade because it worked' — argue from the information available at the time, when the outcome was unknown.

The Red Scare & Life at Home

Quick context Cold War fear came home through McCarthyism, Hollywood blacklists, loyalty oaths, and civil defense drills. Senator McCarthy's accusations destroyed careers based on suspicion rather than evidence, and the line between legitimate security concern and political persecution was often invisible until much later.
analyze

McCarthyism, Hollywood blacklists, and espionage fears all shaped American life in the 1950s. Which had the most damaging effect on American democracy and why?

How to approach this: Pick one and explain the specific democratic norm it damaged — free speech, presumption of innocence, separation of powers. The strongest answer shows how that damage outlasted the specific event.
evaluate

The Vietnam War eventually turned public opinion against the government. What role did the Pentagon Papers, Watergate, and the conduct of the war itself play in that shift? Which was most important?

How to approach this: Rank the three factors and defend your top choice. Each one revealed something different about the government — what did each expose, and which revelation was hardest to absorb for ordinary Americans?
