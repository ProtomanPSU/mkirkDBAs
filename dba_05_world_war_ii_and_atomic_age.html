Causes of WWII & U.S. Neutrality

Quick context WWII grew from the unresolved grievances of WWI, the rise of fascism in Germany and Italy, and the policy of appeasement that allowed Hitler to expand without consequence. The U.S. passed Neutrality Acts to stay out, then quietly supported the Allies through Lend-Lease before Pearl Harbor forced open war in December 1941.
understand

Explain how the Treaty of Versailles, the rise of fascism, and appeasement each contributed to the start of WWII. Which do you think mattered most?

How to approach this: Cover all three in sequence — Versailles created the grievances, fascism channeled them, appeasement removed the obstacles. Then say which link in that chain you think was most decisive.
analyze

The U.S. passed multiple Neutrality Acts but also supplied the Allies through Lend-Lease. At what point did neutrality become fiction? What finally pushed the U.S. into open war?

How to approach this: Track the turning points: Lend-Lease, Atlantic Charter, then Pearl Harbor. At what moment was there no realistic path back to genuine neutrality? Name it specifically.
evaluate

Was America's entry into WWII inevitable after Pearl Harbor, or could a different government have chosen to stay out? What would that have meant for the outcome?

How to approach this: Take a position on inevitability. If you say inevitable, explain what structural forces made staying out impossible. If you say not inevitable, name a realistic alternative path and its likely consequences.

The Home Front & the Holocaust

Quick context On the home front, women entered the workforce, war bonds funded the fight, and propaganda shaped public opinion. At the same time, the U.S. government interned 120,000 Japanese Americans without trial, while knowledge of the Holocaust's scale grew slowly and the U.S. did not bomb the camps or open immigration.
understand

Describe one way the U.S. home front changed during WWII — for industry, for women, or for a specific group of Americans.

How to approach this: Pick one specific change and be concrete — women entering defense industry jobs, rationing and war bonds, Japanese American internment. Then say what that change reveals about the country at the time.
analyze

The U.S. interned 120,000 Japanese Americans but not Italian or German Americans in significant numbers. What fears and assumptions explain that difference? Does the wartime context justify it?

How to approach this: The difference is racial, not just national-origin — make that argument specifically. Then address the justification question directly: does wartime emergency change what a democracy is allowed to do?
create

You are a government official in 1942. Intelligence suggests some members of a particular ancestry group may be disloyal, but most are loyal citizens. You can monitor everyone, restrict movement near bases, intern the group, or protect all rights fully. What do you recommend? What are you risking with each choice?

How to approach this: Name your recommendation and the specific risk you're accepting. The most thoughtful answers acknowledge that every option, including protecting all rights, carries a real cost someone will be held responsible for.

Ending the War & Its Consequences

Quick context The U.S. dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945, ending the Pacific war but opening the nuclear age. WWII's consequences included the Nuremberg Trials, the founding of the United Nations, the Marshall Plan, and the beginning of the Cold War between the U.S. and the Soviet Union.
understand

Name the two atomic bomb targets and explain what happened in the days between the two bombings.

How to approach this: Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9). Between them, Japan's leadership debated surrender while the U.S. waited. Knowing that gap matters for the debate over whether the second bomb was necessary.
analyze

Historians still debate whether dropping the atomic bombs was necessary to end the Pacific war or was partly a message to the Soviet Union. What evidence supports each interpretation, and why can't we settle the question definitively?

How to approach this: Present both cases with specific evidence — casualty estimates, Soviet timing, diplomatic cables. Then explain why the historical question stays open: what evidence would we need to close it, and why don't we have it?
evaluate

WWII produced the Nuremberg Trials, the United Nations, and the rise of two superpowers. Which consequence do you think most shaped the world that followed, and why?

How to approach this: Pick one and trace its effects forward. The superpower rivalry led to the Cold War; the UN created international law norms; Nuremberg established individual accountability. Which thread runs deepest into the present?
