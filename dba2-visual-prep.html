Steel Factory & Industrial Production

Quick context You are in a steel mill, 1885. American factories are producing steel at unprecedented rates. The work is dangerous, exhausting, and transforms the nation's economy. Workers—many immigrant men—labor 12+ hours daily in extreme heat and noise, with minimal safety protection.
understand

Name one technological innovation that changed American manufacturing between 1870 and 1900. How did this innovation increase production?

How to approach this: Think of the Bessemer Process, assembly line concepts, or electric power. Explain specifically how it made production faster or cheaper.
analyze

Factory workers faced danger, long hours, low pay, and no safety standards. Why did workers accept these conditions? What alternatives did they have?

How to approach this: Consider immigrant status, lack of education, need for income, and limited job options. What power did workers actually have?
evaluate

Some argue factories exploited workers but built American wealth; others say workers' suffering was never worth the profit. What's your view?

How to approach this: Take a position and explain what evidence supports it. Consider short-term vs. long-term consequences.

Railroad Boom & Transportation Revolution

Quick context You are at a railroad construction site, 1880. The railroad boom connects the nation—east to west—for the first time. Thousands of workers (many Chinese and Irish immigrants) build the tracks. Railroads transform commerce, settlement patterns, and American identity.
understand

How did railroads change American commerce between 1870 and 1900? Name one specific way railroads affected business or trade.

How to approach this: Consider shipping goods, connecting markets, reaching new customers, or reducing shipping time. Give a concrete example.
analyze

Railroad companies received government land grants and subsidies. Was this a fair use of public resources, or did companies unfairly profit?

How to approach this: Consider what the government got in return, who benefited, and who bore the costs (including Native Americans).
evaluate

Railroads united the country but also enabled the removal of Native Americans and exploitation of natural resources. Were the benefits worth the cost?

How to approach this: Weigh progress against human and environmental harm. Who decides what progress means?
EOC Practice Question

How did the arrival of large numbers of immigrants affect American labor markets?

Correct Answer: B

Why this is correct: The influx of immigrants increased the labor supply, allowing employers to keep wages low and working conditions poor. Desperate for work, immigrants often accepted jobs others refused. This dynamic gave employers significant advantage over workers.

Why the other answers are historically incorrect:
A: Wages actually fell during industrialization due to increased labor supply, not increased scarcity.

C: Immigrants typically worked in the jobs no one else wanted, including dangerous factory positions; they did not refuse factory work.

D: Many labor unions actually excluded or resisted immigrant membership, seeing them as competitors who would accept lower wages.

Tenement City & Urban Growth

Quick context You are in a tenement district, 1895. Millions of immigrants arrive seeking opportunity and jobs in factories. Cities explode in population. Housing is scarce and expensive. Families—often 8-10 people—crowd into tiny apartments with no running water or toilets. Disease spreads rapidly in these conditions.
understand

Why did immigrants choose to live in crowded tenements despite terrible conditions?

How to approach this: Consider limited money, proximity to jobs, community with people from their country, and what alternatives they actually had.
analyze

Tenement conditions—overcrowding, disease, no sanitation—were worst in cities with the most immigrants. Was this because of immigration, or because of how cities were built and managed?

How to approach this: Separate cause from correlation. Who controlled city planning and housing? What choices were made?
evaluate

Some reformers blamed immigrants for city decay; others blamed landlords and city governments. Who was actually responsible?

How to approach this: Consider who had power to change conditions and who chose not to. Distinguish blame from responsibility.
EOC Practice Question

Why was breaking up monopolies considered necessary for a healthy economy?

Correct Answer: B

Why this is correct: While industrialization created wealth, it also created severe urban problems. Factory workers faced long hours, dangerous conditions, and low wages. Cities became overcrowded with tenements and poor sanitation. These conditions sparked labor movements and Progressive Era reforms.

Why the other answers are historically incorrect:
A: Rural areas actually declined economically as people moved to cities for factory work; agriculture became less profitable.

C: Farmers faced low crop prices and often lost land during this period; they did not become wealthy.

D: Factory work benefited factory owners and investors far more than workers; wages were low and conditions were dangerous for most industrial workers.
