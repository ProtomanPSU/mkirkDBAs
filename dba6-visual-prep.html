Origins & Truman's Response

Quick context After WWII, the U.S. and USSR emerged as rival superpowers with incompatible ideologies and competing visions for Europe's future. Truman responded with the Truman Doctrine (aid to countries resisting communism), the Marshall Plan (economic recovery for Western Europe), and NATO (a mutual defense alliance).
Primary Source
From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an Iron Curtain has descended across the continent. Behind that line lie all the capitals of the ancient states of central and eastern Europe. Communist parties have taken control in nearly every one.
Winston Churchill, "Iron Curtain" speech, 1946
Question 1: What is Churchill describing with the image of an "Iron Curtain"? What emotion does that language create?
Question 2: Churchill speaks of "Communist parties taking control." From whose perspective is this statement made? How might it sound from a Soviet perspective?
Myth:
The Soviet Union was expansionist from 1945 onward, driven by communist ideology to conquer the world.
Reality:
The Soviet Union pursued security through control of Eastern Europe, driven by historical experience of invasion from the west (Napoleon, World War II). While Soviet methods were authoritarian, the motive was securing a buffer zone, not necessarily global conquest. Both the US and USSR claimed defensive motives while the other saw aggression.
Interpretation 1
Soviet communism was ideologically driven to expand. Stalin pursued domination of Eastern Europe, supported communist movements globally, and sought to weaken the West. The US containment strategy was the correct response to genuine Soviet aggression.
Traditional Cold War scholars saw Soviet expansion as the primary threat.
Interpretation 2
Both sides pursued security and interpreted the other's actions as threats. Soviet control of Eastern Europe was about creating a buffer. American atomic weapons and encirclement looked like aggression to the Soviets. Mutual fear created a cycle neither side could escape.
Post-Cold War historians examine how both sides' security concerns fed escalation.

How to approach this: Take a clear position. If you say avoidable, name a specific decision point where a different choice could have changed the trajectory. If you say unavoidable, explain which structural factor made cooperation impossible.

Crises, Korea & Vietnam

Quick context The Cold War turned hot in Korea (1950-53) and Vietnam (escalating through the 1960s), while nuclear near-misses like the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) showed how close the superpowers came to direct war. Both Korea and Vietnam ended inconclusively, raising questions about the limits of containment.
Primary Source
My son came home in a flag. He was sent to Korea because of something called "containing communism." I still don't understand what Korea had to do with protecting America. I understand he won't be coming home alive.
Korean War mother's testimony, 1950s
Question 1: Why is this mother confused about why her son is fighting in Korea?
Question 2: Is her confusion reasonable? What was the connection between Korea and American security?
Myth:
America fought in Korea and Vietnam to prevent Soviet domination of those countries and protect democracy.
Reality:
Korea and Vietnam were wars against nationalist and communist forces, but the American interest was containment and preventing geopolitical advantage to the Soviet Union, not necessarily protecting genuine democracy. America often supported authoritarian regimes in these countries while claiming to fight for freedom.
Interpretation 1
American interventions in Korea and Vietnam were attempts to contain Soviet and communist expansion in Asia. If these nations had fallen to communism, the geopolitical balance would have shifted dangerously. American intervention, while costly, prevented worse outcomes.
Traditional Cold War historians justify these interventions as strategically necessary.
Interpretation 2
Korea and Vietnam were civil and nationalist conflicts, not Soviet proxy wars. American intervention in both failed, killing millions, destabilizing the region, and achieving none of its stated goals. These were tragic failures based on misunderstanding local nationalism.
Critical historians argue the US misread these conflicts and intervened disastrously.

The Red Scare & Life at Home

Quick context Cold War fear came home through McCarthyism, Hollywood blacklists, loyalty oaths, and civil defense drills. Senator McCarthy's accusations destroyed careers based on suspicion rather than evidence, and the line between legitimate security concern and political persecution was often invisible until much later.
Primary Source
We conducted air raid drills in elementary school. We hid under our desks as if a wooden desk could protect us from a nuclear bomb. We grew up thinking that at any moment, Soviet missiles would destroy us. I was five years old and I was terrified.
Baby boomer childhood memory, 1950s
Question 1: How did the Cold War's threat of nuclear war shape American childhood and psychology?
Question 2: Did air raid drills make people safer, or were they primarily about managing fear?
Myth:
Cold War nuclear fear was realistic and justified by genuine Soviet threat.
Reality:
The Soviet Union did not have the ability to invade America. Nuclear war was a possibility, but a Soviet invasion was not. Cold War culture exaggerated the threat of nuclear war to maintain public support for military spending and foreign interventions. Fear was politically useful.
Interpretation 1
The Soviet Union had nuclear weapons and hostile intentions. American nuclear fear was rational. Civil defense measures were reasonable precautions. The fear motivated Americans to remain vigilant and support necessary defense spending.
Traditional historians see Cold War fear as realistic and justified.
Interpretation 2
Cold War fear was politically manufactured and out of proportion to actual threat. It was used to justify military spending, foreign interventions, and domestic surveillance. The psychological impact on generations was profound and largely unnecessary.
Cultural historians examine how fear was constructed and used for political purposes.

How to approach this: Pick one and explain the specific democratic norm it damaged — free speech, presumption of innocence, separation of powers. The strongest answer shows how that damage outlasted the specific event.

The Vietnam War eventually turned public opinion against the government. What role did the Pentagon Papers, Watergate, and the conduct of the war itself play in that shift? Which was most important?

How to approach this: Rank the three factors and defend your top choice. Each one revealed something different about the government — what did each expose, and which revelation was hardest to absorb for ordinary Americans?
