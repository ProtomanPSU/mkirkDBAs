Causes of WWII & U.S. Neutrality

Quick context WWII grew from the unresolved grievances of WWI, the rise of fascism in Germany and Italy, and the policy of appeasement that allowed Hitler to expand without consequence. The U.S. passed Neutrality Acts to stay out, then quietly supported the Allies through Lend-Lease before Pearl Harbor forced open war in December 1941.
Primary Source
Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. It was a day of infamy. American servicemen died in their sleep. Our fleet was destroyed. America's response will be swift and terrible.
News reports and President Roosevelt's address, December 7-8, 1941
Question 1: Why does Roosevelt call it a day of infamy? What is the emotional power of that language?
Question 2: Before Pearl Harbor, Americans were divided about entering the war. After Pearl Harbor, support was near-unanimous. Why does a direct attack change everything?
Myth:
The US had to enter World War II after Japan attacked Pearl Harbor.
Reality:
Pearl Harbor was an attack on a military installation, not an invasion of US territory. Technically, the US could have declared war on Japan without immediately entering the European war against Germany. Germany then declared war on the US, making it a true two-front conflict. But the US entry was not inevitable from Pearl Harbor alone.
Interpretation 1
Pearl Harbor was a direct attack on America requiring military response. Japan's invasion of China and Southeast Asia required American opposition. The US had no choice but to fight both Germany and Japan to prevent authoritarian regimes from dominating Europe and Asia.
Most mainstream historians see American entry as strategically necessary and morally justified.
Interpretation 2
Pearl Harbor triggered American entry, but the causes were deeper: oil embargoes that provoked Japan, American support for China, competition for Pacific resources. Japan did not attack a neutral country minding its own business. The war had complex causes on both sides.
Historians examining Japanese perspective note that US actions contributed to Japanese aggression.

The Home Front & the Holocaust

Quick context On the home front, women entered the workforce, war bonds funded the fight, and propaganda shaped public opinion. At the same time, the U.S. government interned 120,000 Japanese Americans without trial, while knowledge of the Holocaust's scale grew slowly and the U.S. did not bomb the camps or open immigration.
Primary Source
In a single flash, the city of Hiroshima was destroyed. Over 70,000 people died instantly. Thousands more burned slowly from radiation. The temperature at ground zero was 7,000 degrees. There was nothing left of them but shadows.
Hiroshima survivor testimony and scientific accounts
Question 1: What does the account of "shadows" tell us about the nature of this weapon?
Question 2: Why drop a weapon that destroys an entire city instantly? What military necessity does that serve?
Myth:
The atomic bomb was necessary to end the war and save American lives by preventing an invasion.
Reality:
Japan's military had already been defeated and was seeking negotiation. The US demand for unconditional surrender—which included keeping the Emperor—was the main obstacle to Japanese surrender. The war could have ended through negotiation without the bomb. Whether the bomb was necessary remains genuinely contested by historians.
Interpretation 1
An invasion of Japan would have cost hundreds of thousands of American lives and possibly millions of Japanese lives. The atomic bomb was a weapon that ended the war instantly. It was horrific, but it saved more lives than it took by preventing prolonged combat.
Military and strategic historians often defend the bomb as the least costly way to end the war.
Interpretation 2
Japan was already defeated and seeking to surrender. The US rejected negotiated peace. The bomb was used not out of military necessity but to intimidate the Soviet Union and demonstrate American power. It was a war crime disguised as military strategy.
Critical historians argue the bomb was politically motivated, not militarily necessary.

Ending the War & Its Consequences

Quick context The U.S. dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945, ending the Pacific war but opening the nuclear age. WWII's consequences included the Nuremberg Trials, the founding of the United Nations, the Marshall Plan, and the beginning of the Cold War between the U.S. and the Soviet Union.
Primary Source
The Soviet threat is existential. Communism seeks to dominate the world. America must contain Soviet expansion by any means necessary. This is not a debate about politics—it is a question of civilization versus barbarism.
Cold War containment rhetoric, late 1940s-1950s
Question 1: How does this framing describe the Soviet Union? What does "contain by any means necessary" mean?
Question 2: What perspectives or concerns does this framing leave out?
Myth:
The Cold War was inevitable after World War II because the US and Soviet Union had incompatible systems.
Reality:
The Cold War developed through specific decisions, misunderstandings, and mutual escalation. Cooperation between the US and Soviet Union was possible immediately after the war. The conflict became inevitable through actions both sides took: Soviet expansionism in Eastern Europe, American atomic weapons strategy, and mutual fear and mistrust.
Interpretation 1
The Soviet Union pursued imperial expansion in Eastern Europe and sought global communist dominance. The US response through containment was justified and necessary. American strategy successfully limited Soviet expansion and eventually led to the Soviet collapse.
Traditional Cold War historians often blame Soviet aggression for starting the conflict.
Interpretation 2
Both superpowers pursued imperial interests and military buildups. The US developed atomic weapons and containment strategy. The Soviets responded by consolidating Eastern Europe and developing their own weapons. Neither side was simply innocent. The conflict was mutually constructed.
Revisionist and post-Cold War historians emphasize shared responsibility and mutual escalation.
