Isolation, Intervention & Empire

Quick context After decades of relative isolation, the U.S. began building an overseas empire in the 1890s through the Spanish-American War, acquiring the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam. American leaders justified expansion through ideas about civilization, markets, and national destiny — while those under U.S. control often saw it differently.
understand

Explain the difference between isolation, intervention, and imperialism. Give one example of each from U.S. history before WWI.

How to approach this: Define each term in one sentence, then give a concrete example. Be specific — 'the U.S. stayed out of European wars' is weaker than naming the Monroe Doctrine or a specific non-intervention.
analyze

American leaders said imperialism spread civilization and opened markets. People in Cuba, the Philippines, and Panama had their own views. Whose perspective does the evidence support more strongly?

How to approach this: Don't split the difference — take a side and support it with specific evidence. What actually happened in the Philippines or Cuba after U.S. control? That's your evidence.
evaluate

Does a powerful nation have the right to control weaker ones if it claims to be helping them? What is the strongest argument for each side?

How to approach this: Present both arguments fairly, then say which one you find more persuasive and why. Your teacher will follow up — so know which side you'd defend.

U.S. Entry into WWI

Quick context The U.S. declared neutrality in 1914 but sold supplies to the Allies and protested German submarine warfare. The sinking of the Lusitania, the Zimmermann Telegram, and unrestricted submarine warfare gradually made neutrality untenable, and the U.S. entered the war in April 1917.
analyze

The U.S. claimed neutrality but sold supplies to the Allies and objected to German submarine warfare. Germany sank ships to cut off those supplies. Who was actually neutral, and who had the stronger argument?

How to approach this: Look at what the U.S. was doing economically, not just what it was saying politically. The strongest answer shows that actions and stated policy pointed in different directions.
create

You are a senator in 1916. The U.S. can stay neutral, quietly support the Allies, or enter the war openly. Each choice has real costs in lives, money, and alliances. Which do you choose and why?

How to approach this: Pick a position and name what you're risking. The best answers don't pretend there's a cost-free option — every choice in this scenario has real downsides.

Aftermath: Treaty & League

Quick context Wilson's 14 Points promised a just peace based on self-determination and a League of Nations, but the Treaty of Versailles punished Germany harshly with reparations and territorial losses. The U.S. Senate refused to ratify the treaty, leaving Wilson's League without its most powerful potential member.
analyze

Wilson's 14 Points called for self-determination and a League of Nations. The Treaty of Versailles punished Germany harshly and ignored many of Wilson's principles. What happened between the vision and the result?

How to approach this: Track the gap: what did Wilson want, what did Britain and France want, and how did the negotiating dynamics produce a result neither fully intended? That gap is where the historical explanation lives.
evaluate

The Senate refused to join the League of Nations. Was that refusal wise, short-sighted, or somewhere in between? What does America's absence mean for how the League functioned?

How to approach this: Take a position and follow its consequences. If you say the refusal was wise, explain what the U.S. avoided. If you say it was short-sighted, explain what the League lost without U.S. membership.
