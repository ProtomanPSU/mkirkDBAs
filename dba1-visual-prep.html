Lincoln's Plans vs. Congress

Quick context Lincoln wanted fast reunification with minimal punishment. Congress wanted to reshape the South and guarantee real freedom for formerly enslaved people. This clash set the stage for the entire Reconstruction era.
Primary Source
A state could rejoin the Union when 10% of its 1860 voters took an oath of loyalty to the Union. High-ranking Confederate officials could not take the oath.
Lincoln's Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction, 1863
Question 1: What does this plan promise about how fast the South could rejoin the Union?
Question 2: Does this plan say anything about what happens to formerly enslaved people? What does its silence tell us?
Myth:
Lincoln wanted to overturn the entire Southern system and give Black Americans immediate equality.
Reality:
Lincoln freed enslaved people as a war measure and opposed slavery's expansion, but his Reconstruction plan was forgiving. His 10% Plan would have readmitted many Southern states with little change to their basic structure. Congress saw this and pushed back, demanding stricter conditions. The radical reconstruction came from Congress, not Lincoln.
The "Lenient" View
Lincoln's 10% Plan would have restored Southern power with minimal accountability. Without Congressional Reconstruction's stricter terms, slavery's legacy would have endured unchallenged.
Historians like Eric Foner argue Lincoln underestimated how deeply slavery's culture shaped Southern politics.
The "Pragmatic" View
Lincoln understood that reconstruction had to happen, and pushing too hard would entrench Southern resistance. A moderate approach could have preserved more of what the war achieved.
Other scholars note Lincoln was navigating a complex political landscape and may have been moving toward stricter terms as the war ended.
Was Lincoln's 10% Plan a reasonable compromise that could have worked, or was it dangerously soft on the South?
Pick a position and explain why you think it's true.
analyze

Compare Lincoln's 10% Plan with the Wade-Davis Bill. What was the key disagreement between Congress and the president about how lenient Reconstruction should be?

How to approach this: Know the core disagreement: Lincoln wanted a fast, lenient return to the Union; Congress wanted stricter conditions and real guarantees for Black Americans. Be ready to say which side had the stronger argument and why.
create

You are advising Congress in 1865. Should Reconstruction be lenient (fast reunion) or strict (enforce real change)? What would you recommend, and what would you be willing to sacrifice for your choice?

How to approach this: Commit to a recommendation. The most convincing answers name what you would gain, what you would lose, and why the tradeoff is worth it — not just which option sounds better in the abstract.

Reconstruction Plans & Amendments

Quick context After the war, Lincoln, Congress, and later President Johnson clashed over how to bring Southern states back into the Union and what rights to guarantee formerly enslaved people. The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments were the era's most lasting legal achievements — and the most fiercely contested.
Primary Source
All persons born or naturalized in the United States...are citizens of the United States and of the State wherein they reside. No State shall make or enforce any law which shall abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens...nor deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.
14th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution, 1868
Question 1: What specific rights does this promise, and to whom?
Question 2: If this was law in 1868, how did Jim Crow segregation happen 28 years later? What went wrong?
Myth:
The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments fixed everything. After 1870, Black Americans had legal equality.
Reality:
The amendments were revolutionary in what they promised, but enforcement was nearly nonexistent. Southern states ignored them. The Supreme Court undermined them. Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) made segregation legal. The 15th Amendment didn't actually protect voting rights until the Voting Rights Act of 1965, nearly 95 years later. Laws are only as strong as the will to enforce them.
The Transformative View
The amendments absolutely worked. They changed what America promised legally. Even though enforcement was slow, they created the legal foundation for every civil rights victory of the 20th century. Brown v. Board of Education was possible only because the 14th Amendment existed.
Constitutional historians note the amendments were genuinely revolutionary, even if the nation took decades to live up to them.
The Failure View
The amendments were a cruel promise. They created a gap between law and reality that lasted nearly a century. For millions of people, they were words on paper while actual life remained unchanged. That's not transformation, it's abandonment.
Some scholars argue the gap between promise and practice was so large it reveals the amendments were more symbolic than functional.
Were the amendments a genuine first step toward freedom, or a broken promise that left Black Americans worse off?
Pick a position and explain why you think it's true.
analyze

The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments guaranteed freedom, citizenship, and voting rights. Which one do you think made the biggest immediate difference for formerly enslaved people? Why?

How to approach this: Don't just list what each amendment did — think about what it meant in practice. Which one put something specific in people's hands right away? Start there, then explain why the others, while important, had more delayed impact.
create

You are advising Congress in 1865. Should Reconstruction emphasize legal guarantees for freedom, or economic support (land, jobs) for formerly enslaved people? What are the tradeoffs?

How to approach this: Commit to a priority. The strongest answer acknowledges what you're choosing NOT to prioritize and why that choice still makes sense.

Collapse of Reconstruction

Quick context Reconstruction ended when federal troops withdrew from the South in 1877, leaving Black Americans exposed to Black Codes, sharecropping, and violent intimidation by groups like the Ku Klux Klan. The period's collapse set the conditions for nearly a century of legal segregation.
Primary Source
Vagrants shall be fined or imprisoned. Former slaves who refused to enter labor contracts at the start of the year shall be arrested and hired out to anyone willing to pay the fine. Violating a labor contract was a criminal offense.
Louisiana Black Code, 1865
Question 1: How is this different from slavery, and how is it the same?
Question 2: The 13th Amendment abolished slavery. How could states legally pass this code in 1865?
Myth:
Reconstruction failed because Black Americans weren't ready for freedom and responsibility.
Reality:
This was a white supremacist argument used to justify the rollback of Black rights. In reality, Black Americans immediately founded schools, churches, and communities. Black men served in Congress and state legislatures competently. The failure of Reconstruction wasn't about Black readiness—it was about white resistance, federal withdrawal, and organized violence.
The Noble Failure View
Reconstruction briefly achieved something extraordinary: Black political participation, federal enforcement of rights, and real opportunity. The failure came not from the policy but from sustained, organized white violence and federal abandonment. It could have worked with stronger commitment.
Historians like Douglas Foner see Reconstruction as proof that equality was possible, but that the nation chose abandonment.
The Doomed Experiment View
Reconstruction never had the resources or sustained political will to fundamentally transform the South. The North wanted reunion more than justice. Once the war ended, Reconstruction's collapse was almost inevitable.
Some scholars argue Reconstruction's failure reveals the structural limits of imposed change without deep cultural shift.
Reconstruction collapsed due to Northern fatigue, Southern violence, and weak federal enforcement. Which factor was MOST responsible for its failure?
Pick a position and explain why you think it's true.
understand

Name two specific ways Black Americans were prevented from exercising their rights after Reconstruction ended — one legal and one through intimidation or violence.

How to approach this: One legal example (Black Codes, poll taxes, literacy tests) and one example of direct violence or intimidation (Klan activity, convict leasing). Knowing both shows you understand the full system, not just its laws.
analyze

After Reconstruction, Black Americans faced political, economic, and social restrictions. Which category of limitation was hardest to fight back against, and why?

How to approach this: Think about which restriction was hardest to fight — political (can't vote), economic (can't build wealth), or social (constant threat of violence). The strongest answer explains why that category also blocked progress in the other two.
evaluate

Reconstruction collapsed due to Northern fatigue, Southern violence, and weak federal enforcement. Which factor was most responsible? What evidence supports your answer?

How to approach this: Pick the one cause you can defend most specifically. Saying 'all three' without ranking them is a non-answer in a live DBA — your teacher will ask you to choose, so choose now and practice defending it.
